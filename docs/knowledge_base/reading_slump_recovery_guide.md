# Reading Slump Recovery Guide

A reference guide to recognizing reading slumps, distinguishing their likely causes, and matching recovery strategies to those causes.

## What a Reading Slump Is

A reading slump is a period where a reader who normally finishes books consistently starts DNFing frequently, reading much more slowly than usual, or avoiding picking up a book at all — despite still wanting to be a reader. It's distinct from a simple change in taste. A slump is usually temporary, situational, and often has an identifiable trigger, even if the reader themselves hasn't articulated it. Treating a slump as a permanent shift in preference (and rebuilding the reader's whole profile around it) is a common and costly mistake — this is precisely why the temporary-vs-durable classification in Reader Modeling exists, and slumps are the clearest case where it matters.

## Common Slump Triggers

Slumps tend to fall into a few recognizable patterns, each of which calls for a different recovery approach:

1. **Post-immersion slump** — the reader just finished a book (or series) they loved intensely, and everything since feels flat by comparison. The problem isn't the new books; it's the comparison. Signal: DNF or low engagement immediately following a very high rating.
2. **Burnout slump** — the reader has been reading heavily, possibly to hit a goal or challenge, and the enjoyment has drained out of the activity itself. Signal: declining engagement across a string of books that were each rated fine, not poorly — the fatigue isn't about book quality.
3. **Mismatch slump** — the reader has drifted into books that don't actually fit their current preferences, often because they're chasing a genre or trend rather than what they've historically enjoyed. Signal: a cluster of DNFs with varied but genre-consistent titles.
4. **Life-context slump** — external stress, time scarcity, or emotional load has reduced reading capacity or appetite, independent of book choice. Signal: reduced reading frequency across the board, not tied to any particular book's qualities, often alongside DNF reasons like "just couldn't focus" rather than content-specific complaints.

Distinguishing these matters because the fix is different for each. Recommending a shorter, lighter book helps a Burnout slump but does nothing for a Post-immersion slump, where the real need is something different enough not to invite comparison.

## Recovery Strategies by Trigger

- **Post-immersion slump:** recommend something meaningfully different in mood, tone, or trope from the book that caused the high — not a direct genre substitute, which will only invite unfavorable comparison. The goal is resetting the reader's frame of reference, not finding "the next best thing."
- **Burnout slump:** recommend something shorter, lower-commitment, or in a mood/tone the reader has rated well but that requires less sustained attention — a Cozy or Brisk-paced book with low emotional demand, regardless of genre. The goal is rebuilding momentum with an easy win, not maintaining reading volume.
- **Mismatch slump:** recommend a return to the reader's highest-confidence historical preferences (their most reliable pacing, mood, and trope signals) rather than continuing to explore adjacent or trending territory. The goal is re-anchoring to what's actually worked before.
- **Life-context slump:** recommend something very low-commitment and forgiving of interrupted reading — short chapters, minimal plot threads to track, low emotional intensity — and consider surfacing this as a mood/pacing question rather than guessing, since context signals are the least reliable to infer silently.

## Slumps Should Not Overwrite the Reader Model

This is the most important operational rule in this guide: a slump's low ratings and DNFs should not be treated as ordinary preference signals and folded into confidence scores at full weight. A reader who DNFs three books during a Life-context slump has not developed an aversion to those books' tropes or genres — the DNFs are situational, not preferential. Reader Modeling should down-weight or flag signals gathered during a suspected slump window rather than let them silently erode confidence in previously well-established preferences. Determining that a slump is likely underway is itself a signal worth surfacing to the Planner, since it changes how every subsequent piece of feedback should be interpreted.

## Recognizing a Slump vs. a Genuine Preference Shift

Not every run of DNFs is a slump — sometimes a reader's taste has actually changed, and treating a real shift as "just a slump" under-serves them just as badly as the reverse. A few distinguishing signals:

- A slump tends to resolve once the right book is found — one strong recommendation restores normal engagement. A genuine preference shift persists even across well-matched recommendations for the *old* preference profile.
- A slump usually has no consistent new preference emerging in its place — the reader isn't gravitating toward anything, just away from everything. A genuine shift often shows a new, consistent pattern (e.g., a reader consistently praising Contemplative pacing after previously preferring Breakneck).
- Life-context clues in a reader's own language ("things have been a lot lately," "I just haven't had the headspace") are a much stronger indicator of a slump than a silent string of low ratings with no stated reason — this is a case where explicit free-text signal should outweigh a purely behavioral pattern.

When it's genuinely ambiguous, the safer default is to treat it as a slump first: down-weighting a few data points that turn out to reflect a real shift is a smaller error than overwriting a reader's established profile based on what was actually a temporary rough patch.
