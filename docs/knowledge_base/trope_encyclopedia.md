# Trope Encyclopedia

A reference guide to common narrative tropes, how they cluster, and how reader reactions to them should be interpreted.

## What a Trope Is (and Isn't)

A trope is a recurring narrative device, character type, or plot structure that readers recognize across books — "chosen one," "found family," "unreliable narrator," "heist crew with one job left." Tropes are not inherently good or bad, and they are not the same as genre. A trope is a *pattern within* a story; a genre is a *category of* story. The same trope shows up across genres in different clothing: a "mentor-student bond" appears in fantasy, literary fiction, and workplace-drama novels alike.

This matters for the agent because trope preference is often the most durable and specific signal available — more specific than genre, and often more predictive of satisfaction than genre is. A reader who says "I don't usually read fantasy, but I love a good found-family story" is not being contradictory; they're expressing a trope preference that cuts across their stated genre preference. The Reader Model should be able to hold both facts without treating them as a conflict to resolve.

## Trope Categories

For consistency, tropes are grouped into five functional categories:

1. **Relational tropes** — govern how characters' bonds form or shift over the course of a story: found family, mentor-student bond, rivals-to-allies, unlikely alliance, betrayal-and-reconciliation.
2. **Structural tropes** — govern the shape of the plot itself: chosen one, time loop, heist, locked-room mystery, multiple POV converging on a single event.
3. **Identity tropes** — center on a character's self-discovery or concealment: secret royalty, fish-out-of-water, redemption arc, morally-gray protagonist, unreliable narrator.
4. **World tropes** — concern the setting's rules and how they're revealed: magic system with a cost, dying world, post-apocalyptic rebuild, portal to another world, hidden society operating behind ordinary life.
5. **Tone tropes** — shape the emotional register more than the plot: found-family comfort read, grimdark subversion of a familiar genre, cozy small-stakes mystery, slow-build tension toward a single reveal.

## Trope vs. Pacing: A Common Confusion

Some tropes describe a *rate of development* rather than an event, and these are easy to conflate with pacing. "Slow-build" as a trope (a bond, rivalry, or reveal that develops gradually across the whole book) is not the same as "Slow-burn" pacing in the Pacing Taxonomy (the overall speed of plot events). A book can have Breakneck plot pacing — constant action, escalating stakes — while a central relationship or mystery is still a slow-build trope, unresolved until very late. When parsing reader feedback, phrases like "I want more of a slow build" usually describe how a specific relationship or reveal unfolds, not how fast the plot overall moves — the agent should default to that reading unless the phrasing clearly refers to plot speed in general.

## How Trope Preferences Should Be Weighted

Trope preferences differ from pacing preferences in one important way: they are rarely near-hard filters. A reader who loves found-family stories will still often enjoy a book without one, whereas a reader with a high-confidence Breakneck pacing preference will actively bounce off a Measured book. This means the Planner should treat trope match as an additive positive signal — it raises confidence in a recommendation and is worth surfacing as a reason ("this has the found-family dynamic you've liked before") — but a trope mismatch alone shouldn't disqualify a recommendation the way a pacing mismatch can.

The exception is trope *aversions*, which should be weighted more like a filter than a preference. A reader who has explicitly said they dislike or actively avoid a trope (common examples: a plot twist that hinges on a character's betrayal for shock value, a character death used purely for shock value, an ending that resolves the central mystery through coincidence rather than earned reveal) should have that treated with high weight against recommendation, because negative trope reactions tend to be stronger and more durable than positive ones — readers DNF over an aversion far more reliably than they DNF from the mere absence of a liked trope.

## Signals From Free Text

Reader Modeling should watch for trope mentions in both directions:

- Positive: "I loved how the crew finally trusted each other by the end," "the mentor figure in this book made the whole story," "I'm such a sucker for a redemption arc" — these should raise confidence on the named trope category as a positive preference.
- Negative: "I couldn't get past how the twist relied on a random coincidence," "I DNF'd because a character died just for shock value," "not another chosen-one story" — these should be treated as aversions, weighted more heavily per the section above, and should default to durable unless later contradicted.
- Ambiguous or genre-flavored phrasing ("I like when fantasy books have real stakes") often encodes a structural trope preference (world tropes with consequence, or morally-gray protagonists) rather than a genre statement, and should be parsed for the underlying trope rather than taken at face value as a genre comment.

## Tropes and Series Continuity

Trope preference is one of the few signals that should carry forward strongly within a series — if a reader loved the found family dynamic in book one of a series, that's not new information by book three; it's a confirmed, high-confidence preference that doesn't need to be re-inferred.
